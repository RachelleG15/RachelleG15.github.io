Case Studies

Case 01 — Turnaround

Loss-Making Branch to Profitable in Five Months

Context. Team Manager. One branch was underperforming against target and losing money. The cause was not a single broken thing: coverage did not match demand, pricing was not positioned against the market the branch actually served, and transaction volumes were too thin to carry the cost base.

What I did.

  • Diagnosed before acting. Analysed the target market and reviewed what needed to change — demand, pricing, and the branch's cost base — so the turnaround was built on what the analysis showed rather than assumption.
  • Repriced and cut what wasn't earning. Adjusted pricing against the branch's market position and put cost-effective initiatives in place, so transaction volume could carry the cost base rather than the other way round.
  • Rostered the right staff to demand. Rebuilt the roster against actual business demand rather than a fixed pattern, putting the right people on the floor when customers arrived.
  • Went to where the customers were. Spoke directly with camp managers to reach the expatriate labour communities that made up the transaction base, and built ongoing marketing relationships through them.
  • Held the service line. Kept service quality and compliance standards intact throughout — the branch could not absorb a quality incident while rebuilding volume.

Outcome. The branch moved from loss-making to profitable inside approximately five months, and has consistently met its monthly target since, with transaction volumes repeatedly breaking previous records month-over-month.

What I'd do differently: I diagnosed the branch across market, pricing, cost, and coverage and fixed them together, which is what a turnaround needs. But I committed the roster change across the whole branch in one move, because the demand analysis was clear and I wanted coverage right immediately. I'd still fix pricing and cost fast; I'd pilot the rostering on a single shift for two or three weeks first. The staff adapted, but a gradual change would have cost less goodwill — and goodwill is a resource you spend quickly during a turnaround.

Case 02 — Process Design

SOP for Remittance Operations

Context. Remittance Customer Support Manager. Frontline staff were handling flows without a shared reference — new joiners learned from whoever was beside them, and the same process could be executed two different ways on two different days. In a remittance environment that is a compliance exposure as much as a service one.

What I did. Wrote the remittance operations SOP end-to-end so the flows were documented and understood rather than tribal knowledge. Trained the frontliners on it, translating each step into the remittance perspective — what it means for the customer, for the transaction, and for the AML/KYC obligation at that point in the process. Kept the SOP as the standing reference for onboarding and continuous training.

Outcome. Remittance handling moved from individual interpretation to a single documented standard, with new frontliners trained against the same reference as experienced staff and compliance expectations understood at each step rather than applied after the fact.

What I'd do differently: I wrote the SOP as the reference document and only then trained against it. I'd reverse that sequence next time and sit with the frontline while writing the first draft — the steps they described informally were sometimes better than the ones I'd documented, and I'd have captured that before the standard was fixed.

Case 03 — Systems

API Testing for the New POS Rollout

Context. The business was introducing a new POS system. Rollouts like this tend to fail at the counter rather than in development — if the frontline discovers the faults, the customer discovers them at the same moment.

What I did. Worked within the API testing team for the new POS, testing the integration from the operational side rather than only the technical one. Brought the frontline view into testing: the failure modes that matter at a counter under volume, not only the ones that matter in a test case. Fed findings back ahead of rollout so issues were resolved before customer exposure.

Outcome. Integration and edge-case issues were identified and resolved before launch rather than surfacing on the floor, and the rollout went out with frontline workflow requirements already accounted for.

What I'd do differently: I joined testing after the test cases were largely written, so my contribution was shaped by what had already been defined. I'd push to be in the requirements conversation at the start, because the operational failure modes I ended up flagging are cheapest to design around before a test plan exists.

Case 04 — Product

Onboarding New Products on Demand

Context. Customer demand was signalling products the business was not offering, and the support function was absorbing the cost of that gap through repeated queries and lost transactions.

What I did. Identified demand from customer contact patterns and market signals rather than assumption. Onboarded new products against that demand, taking each one from decision through to frontline-ready, and trained the support team so the products could be handled and explained confidently from day one.

Outcome. The business began offering products its customer base was already asking for, with the support team able to handle and explain them from launch rather than learning them in front of customers.

What I'd do differently: I onboarded products against demand I could see in our own contact patterns — which is real signal, but it's a rear-view mirror. I'd put a short structured feedback loop in front of customers before committing the team's training time, so demand came from asking rather than inferring.

Case 05 — Leadership

KPI Reporting & Workforce Scheduling

Context. Team Manager across operational units. Leadership needed a live view of service quality and throughput rather than periodic summaries, and the floor needed staffing that tracked demand instead of the calendar.

What I did. Designed and modified rostering against business demand to align staffing with real-time volume, including dynamic reallocation as demand moved through the day. Managed team KPIs and ran regular coaching sessions and evaluations tied to those measures, so performance conversations rested on evidence. Generated executive-level KPI dashboards, operational reports, and customer feedback summaries. Enforced AML/CFT standards across all transactions on the floor, and handled critical customer concerns directly — high empathy, speed, and accuracy under volume.

Outcome. Staffing tracked actual demand rather than a fixed pattern, performance management ran on consistent KPI evidence, and leadership took service and resourcing decisions from a live operational view rather than retrospective reporting.

What I'd do differently: I built the dashboards to answer the questions leadership had already asked, which made them useful but reactive. I'd want to know which decisions the numbers were meant to change before designing the reports, and then design backwards from that.
